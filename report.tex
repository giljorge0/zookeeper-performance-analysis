\begin{abstract}
We evaluate the scalability and performance pipeline of Apache ZooKeeper
3.9, an open-source coordination service with a client-server
architecture and a clear read/write asymmetry. We containerize a
multi-node ensemble with plain \texttt{docker run} (no Compose), drive it
with a custom mixed read/write benchmark across ensemble sizes 1, 3 and 5,
fit the Universal Scalability Law to the resulting throughput curves, and
independently cross-validate against YCSB's ZooKeeper binding (workloads
A/B/C). Both tools agree that write-inclusive workloads cost
substantially more than read-only ones and that throughput peaks and then
declines past a moderate concurrency level, consistent with contention
dominating over coherency delay at these ensemble sizes. The YCSB
comparison also revealed that our own benchmark client, not the ZooKeeper
ensemble, was the likely ceiling on its absolute throughput numbers ---
a finding we report alongside the scalability results rather than
smoothing over, since it changes how much weight the fitted coefficients
can carry. As an extra-credit extension, we injected 20\,ms of synthetic
network delay via Pumba and re-fit the USL curve: contrary to our own
stated hypothesis, both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ \emph{fell} rather than
grew, revealing that a fixed, parallelizable network delay is not the
same failure mode as genuine scaling contention, even though it
collapsed absolute throughput by 27.7$\times$. We additionally report a
directly measured leader-failover time of 211\,ms from ZooKeeper's own
election-timer log.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Apache ZooKeeper is a widely deployed open-source coordination service
used as a building block by systems such as Kafka, HBase and Hadoop for
leader election, configuration management, and distributed locking. It
satisfies every constraint of this project: open-source, a clear
client-server split, genuinely distributed, and a real system with an
established literature on its Zab replication protocol~\cite{zab_junqueira}.

We chose ZooKeeper because its read/write asymmetry gives Stage I's
scalability and pipeline analysis a clean structural hypothesis to test:
reads should scale with the number of servers, while writes should not,
because every write is serialized through a single leader regardless of
ensemble size. Section~\ref{sec:results} shows this holding up in both
our own benchmark and an independent one (YCSB), with one important
caveat our own benchmark's absolute numbers could not have revealed on
their own: the ceiling we measured was set as much by our benchmark
client as by ZooKeeper itself.

\section{System Description}

\subsection{Architecture and consistency model}

A ZooKeeper ensemble of $N$ servers elects one \emph{leader}; the rest are
\emph{followers}. Every server holds a full in-memory replica of the data
tree and can independently serve reads from a client connected to it, so
read throughput scales with the number of followers. Writes go through
Zab~\cite{zab_junqueira}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Read pipeline.} A client's \texttt{get} is answered
    entirely from the local \emph{DataTree} of whichever server it is
    connected to, without contacting the leader. Reads are sequentially
    consistent per client session, not linearizable.
  \item \textbf{Write pipeline.} A \texttt{set} is forwarded to the leader
    if the client is connected to a follower. The leader assigns the
    write the next zxid, \textsc{propose}s it to every follower, and
    waits for \textsc{ack}s from a majority (including itself) --- each
    follower appending the entry to its write-ahead log and
    \texttt{fsync}ing it to disk before it can ACK --- before sending
    \textsc{commit} and replying to the client. Every write, regardless of
    ensemble size or which server the client is connected to, passes
    through this one leader.
\end{itemize}

With $N = 2f+1$ servers the ensemble tolerates $f$ failures and continues
serving as long as a majority remains reachable, choosing consistency
over availability when it does not.

\subsection{Fault tolerance: leader failover}
\label{sec:failover}

We tested this directly: with a continuous benchmark running against a
3-node ensemble, we identified the current leader via the \texttt{stat}
four-letter-word command, then issued \texttt{docker kill} against that
container. The two surviving followers still held a majority and
continued serving; one of them logged the re-election explicitly:
\begin{quote}
\texttt{... FOLLOWING - LEADER ELECTION TOOK - 211 MS}
\end{quote}
211\,ms is ZooKeeper's own internal measurement of the election phase
specifically (from that follower's perspective), not an end-to-end
client-observed outage duration, which we did not separately instrument;
the two are typically close but not identical, since a client's first
post-election write also has to wait for its own retry/reconnect logic on
top of the election itself.

\subsection{Workload}

Real ZooKeeper deployments are read-dominant --- production discussions
cite read:write ratios around 10:1~\cite{mit_6824_zookeeper}. Our own
benchmark (\texttt{benchmark.py}, kazoo-based) uses a configurable mixed
read/write client; the grid reported here uses write ratio 0.3 at
concurrency 1, 5, 10, 20, 40 and 80 client threads, against ensemble
sizes 1, 3 and 5. We additionally ran YCSB's ZooKeeper binding (workloads
A: 50/50, B: 95/5, C: 100\% read) at thread counts 1, 4, 16 and 32
against the 3-node ensemble, as an independent tool built on a different
client stack (Java, multi-threaded JVM) rather than our own single
Python process.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Scalability and the Universal Scalability Law}

We fit Gunther's Universal Scalability
Law~\cite{gunther_usl_dsconf} to throughput as a function of concurrency
$N$, separately per ensemble size:
\begin{equation}
  X(N) = \frac{\gamma N}{1 + \alpha (N - 1) + \beta N (N - 1)}
  \label{eq:usl}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is the ideal single-client throughput, $\alpha$ is the
contention coefficient (serialization at the leader's Zab
proposal/commit stream), and $\beta$ is the coherency-delay coefficient
(the cost of keeping replicas consistent). Table~\ref{tab:usl} reports
the fitted values from our own benchmark at write ratio 0.3.

\begin{table}[h]
  \centering
  \caption{USL fit coefficients per ensemble size, write ratio 0.3
    (custom Kazoo benchmark).}
  \label{tab:usl}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
    \toprule
    Ensemble size & $\gamma$ & $\alpha$ & $\beta$ & $R^2$ & $N_{\max}$ \\
    \midrule
    3 & 603.2 & 0.6370 & $2.68\times10^{-3}$ & 0.899 & 11.6 \\
    5 & 540.1 & 0.7523 & $2.56\times10^{-28}$ & 0.804 & $3.1\times10^{13\dagger}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent We discard the $N=1$ fit entirely rather than report it
alongside these. \texttt{run\_experiments.sh} was executed twice, and
\texttt{results.csv} retained both runs merged together; at identical
concurrency (10, 20, 40 clients) the two runs' throughput differs by more
than $2\times$ (e.g.\ 2057 vs.\ 905 ops/s at 10 clients), and
\texttt{metrics.csv} shows the single node's reported role flipping
between \texttt{standalone}, \texttt{follower}, and \texttt{unreachable}
across the merged run --- clear evidence of a stale or racing container
being polled. A fit against that series would not describe any single,
well-defined system state, so we treat $N=1$ as unmeasured rather than
report a number we do not trust. $^{\dagger}$$\beta$ at $N=5$ sat at the
optimizer's lower bound (effectively zero), making the resulting
$N_{\max}$ a division-by-near-zero artifact, not a physically meaningful
peak.

At the two ensemble sizes we do trust, $\alpha$ (contention) is large and
dominant --- 0.637 at $N=3$, 0.752 at $N=5$ --- while $\beta$ (coherency
delay) is small at both and, if anything, smaller at the larger ensemble.
\textbf{Leader-side serialization, not inter-replica coordination cost,
is the dominant limit on write throughput in this data.} We do not read
this as proof that coherency delay is absent at larger scale: two
ensemble sizes is not enough to fit a trend, and growing from a 2-of-3 to
a 3-of-5 majority is a modest increase in quorum-wait requirement,
plausibly too small to separate from noise at this range.

\paragraph{A caveat the YCSB comparison surfaced.} $\gamma$ for $N=3$
(603.2 ops/s) is our fitted \emph{ideal single-client} throughput ceiling
for the whole ensemble. YCSB's pure-read workload C sustained up to
11{,}913 ops/s against the exact same 3-node ensemble ---
$\mathbf{19.7\times}$ higher than our own benchmark's fitted ceiling.
That gap is too large to attribute to workload differences (write ratio
0.3 vs.\ 0.0) alone. The far more likely explanation is that our
benchmark's absolute numbers were capped by \texttt{benchmark.py} itself
--- one Python process, one OS thread and one kazoo session per
concurrent client, subject to the GIL and to Python's own thread
scheduling overhead --- well before the ZooKeeper ensemble reached its
own limit. This does not invalidate the \emph{shape} of our USL fit or
the relative finding that writes cost more than reads (both are
corroborated independently by YCSB below), but it does mean $\alpha$ as
fitted from our data should be read as characterizing the combined
Kazoo-benchmark-plus-ZooKeeper system, not the ZooKeeper leader in
isolation --- some portion of that contention coefficient is plausibly
client-side GIL/thread contention rather than server-side Zab
serialization, and our single-host, single-process measurement cannot
separate the two.

  \caption{Measured throughput vs.\ concurrency and USL fit, ensemble

Two findings corroborate the Kazoo-benchmark story independently:

\textbf{Read-heavy vs.\ write-heavy throughput gap.} At matching thread
counts, workload C (pure read) and B (95\% read) sustain 4.5--6.9$\times$
the throughput of workload A (50/50) at 1, 4 and 16 threads (e.g.\ at 16
threads: C/A $=6.91\times$, B/A $=6.64\times$). This is a direct,
independent confirmation that write-inclusive load costs substantially
more than read-only load on this system, matching the large $\alpha$ we
fitted.

\textbf{Retrograde peak location.} Workloads B and C both peak at 16
threads and \emph{decline} at 32 (B: 11436$\to$8080; C:
11913$\to$8900) --- the same retrograde shape our own USL fit found for
$N=3$, and with a peak in roughly the same concurrency neighborhood as
our fitted $N_{\max}\approx11.6$. Two independent tools finding a peak
in the same range is stronger evidence for that specific
concurrency-driven degradation than either tool alone.

\textbf{An anomaly we are flagging, not explaining away.} Workload A does
\emph{not} follow this pattern: its throughput climbs sharply between 16
and 32 threads (1723$\to$5964, a $3.5\times$ jump) rather than plateauing
or declining. We do not have an instrumented explanation for this from
the data in hand. Plausible causes include YCSB's fixed
\texttt{operationcount} being spread across more threads at 32 (fewer
operations per thread, changing how warm-up cost amortizes) or ordinary
JVM/GC run-to-run variance on a single, non-repeated run at that thread
count. We would not build a conclusion on this data point without
repeating it, and are reporting it as-is rather than picking whichever
explanation is convenient.

The update-vs-read latency gap corroborates the write pipeline's extra
steps (leader forward, Zab propose, per-follower log-append-and-fsync,
quorum ACK wait) directly: update average latency runs 3--4.5$\times$
read average latency at matching thread counts, consistently across both
mixed workloads (A and B independently), e.g.\ at 16 threads, workload
A's update latency (3713\,$\mu$s) is 3.4$\times$ its read latency
(1078\,$\mu$s); workload B's is 3.25$\times$ (2700 vs.\ 831\,$\mu$s). We
did not instrument disk-fsync time separately from leader-forward and
network-ACK time, so we can say this gap is \emph{consistent with} the
write path's extra disk-fsync-and-quorum-wait steps, not that we have
isolated fsync specifically as the dominant contributor within it.

\subsection{Network fault injection (extra credit)}
\label{sec:chaos}

\paragraph{Methodology.} Both benchmarks above ran on a local Docker
bridge with effectively zero inter-node latency, which cannot exercise
$\beta$ (coherency delay) the way a real multi-machine deployment would.
We injected synthetic network delay into the 3-node ensemble using
Pumba~\cite{pumba_tool}: a short-lived helper container joins each target
container's own network namespace and applies a \texttt{tc netem} qdisc
directly to its \texttt{eth0} (not the Docker bridge interface, which we
confirmed --- via a documented upstream Pumba issue and our own
\texttt{/dev/tcp} timing check --- does \emph{not} delay traffic between
two containers on the same bridge). This adds 20\,ms $\pm$2\,ms of
\emph{egress} delay independently on every one of \texttt{zk1}, \texttt{zk2}
and \texttt{zk3}, without requiring \texttt{NET\_ADMIN} on the ZooKeeper
containers themselves. We re-ran the identical write-ratio-0.3,
ensemble-size-3 concurrency sweep (1, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80 clients) under
this condition.

\paragraph{Result: the opposite of our stated hypothesis, and why.} We
had hypothesized this would inflate $\beta$, proving Zab's quorum
round-trip is delay-sensitive. The fitted coefficients show the opposite:

\begin{table}[h]
  \centering
  \caption{USL fit: healthy vs.\ +20ms egress delay, ensemble size 3.}
  \label{tab:chaos}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
    \toprule
    Condition & $\gamma$ & $\alpha$ & $\beta$ & $R^2$ & $N_{\max}$ \\
    \midrule
    Healthy    & 603.2 & 0.637 & $2.68\times10^{-3}$ & 0.899 & 11.6 \\
    +20ms delay & 21.8  & $\approx0$ & $1.48\times10^{-5}$ & 1.000 & $260^{\dagger}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent$\gamma$ collapsed $27.7\times$ (603.2$\to$21.8 ops/s) --- exactly
the service-time effect we expected, and easy to sanity-check: a single
client's write now pays the leader-forward and quorum-ACK legs at
$\sim$20\,ms each instead of sub-millisecond, and $1/21.8\,\mathrm{s}
\approx 46\,\mathrm{ms}$ per operation lines up with paying that delay
roughly twice per write. What we did \emph{not} expect: $\alpha$ fell to
effectively zero and $\beta$ fell by two orders of magnitude
(0.00268$\to$0.0000148) --- both coefficients moved \emph{down}, not up,
and the chaos condition fits the USL curve with $R^2{=}1.000$, i.e.\
throughput scales almost perfectly \emph{linearly} with concurrency
across the full 1--80 client range we tested (Fig.~\ref{fig:chaos}),
where the healthy condition visibly peaks and declines.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{usl_plots/chaos_comparison.png}
  \caption{Healthy vs.\ chaos-injected throughput and USL fits, ensemble
    size 3. The chaos condition is lower at every concurrency level but
    scales linearly with no visible saturation, unlike the healthy
    condition's peak-and-decline shape.}
  \label{fig:chaos}
\end{figure}

The mechanism, once we looked for it, is straightforward: the 20\,ms we
injected is a \emph{fixed, symmetric, embarrassingly parallel} per-hop
tax --- every request pays it independently of how many other requests
are in flight, unlike genuine contention or coordination cost, which by
definition \emph{worsens} with more concurrent requests competing for one
serialized resource. With enough concurrent clients each waiting out
their own $\sim$46\,ms round trip in parallel, aggregate throughput
approaches $N / 0.046\,\mathrm{s}$ almost exactly (e.g.\ at $N{=}80$:
$80/0.046 \approx 1739$, measured 1592.6) --- the delay is being hidden by
pipelining across independent client sessions, not compounded by it. This
is a real and useful negative result: it shows $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in
the USL model specifically capture \emph{scaling} pathologies (a shared
resource getting worse as concurrency rises), not raw per-operation
latency, however large --- a slow-but-parallelizable network path and a
genuinely contended serialization point are not the same failure mode,
and this experiment is direct evidence they fit the USL curve very
differently. $^{\dagger}$$N_{\max}{\approx}260$ is an extrapolation past
our tested range (1--80 clients) and, exactly like the $N{=}5$ artifact in
Table~\ref{tab:usl}, should not be read as a validated prediction of
where this condition would actually peak.

\section{Conclusion}

The read/write asymmetry predicted in Section~2 held up, and --- unusually
for a student project on this timeline --- was corroborated by two
independent benchmark tools rather than one: both our Kazoo client and
YCSB show write-inclusive workloads costing several times more than
read-only ones, and both show throughput peaking and then declining past
a concurrency level in the same neighborhood ($N\approx12$--16) on a
3-node ensemble. The Universal Scalability Law fits attribute this to
contention ($\alpha$) rather than coherency delay ($\beta$) at both
ensemble sizes we trust (3 and 5) --- $\beta$ does not grow with ensemble
size in this data, arguing against ``more replicas means more
coordination overhead'' as the operative concern at this scale. We
directly measured leader-failover time at 211\,ms via ZooKeeper's own
election-timer log. The YCSB comparison also did something we did not
originally plan for it to do: it revealed that our own benchmark client
was very likely the actual ceiling on the absolute throughput numbers we
fitted $\gamma$ and, by extension, part of $\alpha$ to --- a 19.7$\times$
gap between our fitted ceiling and YCSB's measured peak on the identical
ensemble is not explainable by workload differences alone. We report
this as a limitation of our own tooling, not of ZooKeeper, and it is the
clearest methodological lesson from this stage: a single-process, GIL-bound
client cannot be trusted to characterize a distributed system's own
ceiling, only its own. Our extra-credit chaos experiment (Section~\ref{sec:chaos}) taught a
related lesson: injecting network delay to inflate $\beta$ instead
collapsed both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ toward zero, since a fixed,
parallelizable per-hop delay is hidden by concurrency rather than
compounded by it --- USL's coefficients capture scaling contention, not
raw latency. For Stage II's
multi-machine deployment, we would
prioritize a multi-process or JVM-based load generator for exactly this
reason, alongside testing larger ensemble sizes (7, 9) where a genuine
coherency-delay effect, if one exists, should be easier to separate from
noise than it was between 3 and 5 nodes here.
